\begin{abstract}
%==============================================================================
High-Bandwidth Flash (HBF) offers roughly $16\times$ the capacity of HBM at
comparable bandwidth, and H3 places it behind HBM in a daisy-chained hierarchy.
H3 confines HBF to read-only data because NAND write endurance is the
technology's weak point. We study the write-heavy case it excludes: KV blocks
spilled from HBM into a store backed by HBF. We ask whether a small
non-volatile staging tier of phase-change memory (PCM), stacked on each HBF
stack within its footprint (\SI{43.4}{\gibi\byte} per GPU), can absorb that
write stream.

We simulate an 8-GPU LLaMA2-70B-GQA cluster replaying the full Mooncake FAST'25
traces in a discrete-event simulator, and keep three claims separate.
\emph{First, an identity:} removing the baseline's write-through copy halves
flash writes (\SI{50.2}{\percent}), regardless of flash capacity. This is a
structural cost of the baseline, not an effect of the PCM medium.
\emph{Second, an argument we do not measure:} the staging tier should be
non-volatile because, under write-back, acknowledged data lives only there.
\emph{Third, our contribution:} read-gated demotion, which sends a block to
flash only if PCM actually served it. It removes \SIrange{98.7}{99.0}{\percent}
of the remaining flash writes (at least \SI{99.33}{\percent} against the
baseline). The price is reuse: measured against a no-store control, it keeps
only \SIrange{9}{15}{\percent} of the store's reuse benefit. We price its PCM
wear as endurance thresholds. Read-gating outlasts write-back alone above about
$2.4\times10^{5}$ PCM cycles, and gains nothing further above
\numrange{1.8e7}{2.5e7}. No endurance figure is published for the PCM
generation whose density we use, so we report thresholds rather than a
verdict. We also list the corrections to earlier versions of this work and what
we do not claim.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
LLM inference, KV cache, high-bandwidth flash, phase-change memory, write
endurance, memory hierarchy.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

%==============================================================================
\section{Introduction}
%==============================================================================
The key--value (KV) cache of a transformer dominates the memory budget of
large-language-model (LLM) serving. At LLaMA2-70B with grouped-query attention,
tensor-parallel degree two, and FP16 precision, a single token costs
\SI{163840}{\byte} of KV state per tensor-parallel shard; a \num{13000}-token
prompt therefore occupies roughly \SI{2}{\gibi\byte} per shard before a single
token is generated. HBM capacity, not
compute, is what bounds concurrency.

High-Bandwidth Flash (HBF) is a recent response: a NAND stack built to HBM-like
bandwidth and placed in the same package, offering on the order of $16\times$
the capacity. The H3 proposal~\cite{h3} daisy-chains HBF behind HBM, routing
through an address decoder in the HBM base die and hiding flash's microsecond
read latency behind a \SI{40}{\mega\byte} SRAM prefetch buffer. Crucially, H3
confines HBF to \emph{read-only} data---weights and shared, pre-computed KV---and
argues on that basis that NAND's limited write endurance is not a disadvantage.

That argument does not survive contact with general serving. When the flash tier
backs a KV store that absorbs spill from HBM, it receives a sustained write
stream, and TLC NAND rated at \num{3000} program/erase cycles must absorb it.
This paper asks how much of that stream a small \emph{bit-alterable}
non-volatile tier---phase-change memory---between HBM and HBF can absorb, what
it costs in reuse, and what PCM endurance it requires.

\paragraph{Contributions}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A PCM staging tier sized from the sourced first-generation 3D XPoint
        density of \SI{0.62}{\giga\bit\per\milli\meter\squared}~\cite{ehrmann2022pcm}
        (\SI{43.4}{\gibi\byte} per GPU), placed on the HBF stacks within their
        footprint (\S\ref{sec:arch}).
  \item A store model in which PCM is a true level of the hierarchy: spill-only
        entry, lookup beyond the HBM hit, and read credit only on an actual PCM
        serve. We also report how each simpler alternative fails
        (\S\ref{sec:storemodel}).
  \item A measurement of the baseline's structural cost: write-through spends
        one extra flash write per admitted block, and removing it halves flash
        writes regardless of flash capacity. We present this as characterising
        the baseline, not as a contribution of PCM (\S\ref{sec:identity}).
  \item Read-gated demotion: about \SI{99}{\percent} of the remaining flash
        writes removed, at the cost of most of the store's reuse benefit,
        measured against a no-store control (\S\ref{sec:headline}).
  \item PCM wear priced as endurance thresholds over a swept axis, with every
        assumption stated (\S\ref{sec:endurance}).
  \item The corrections to earlier versions of this work, and an explicit
        statement of what is not claimed (\S\ref{sec:limits}).
\end{itemize}

%==============================================================================
\section{Background and Motivation}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{KV cache offload}
Production serving systems already treat the KV cache as a storage hierarchy.
vLLM's paged attention~\cite{vllm} manages HBM in fixed-size blocks and reuses
shared prefixes across requests; Mooncake~\cite{mooncake} extends this to a
disaggregated store with a DRAM memory tier and an SSD offload tier, admitting
blocks on prefill and evicting by LRU. The economics are attractive because
prompts are heavily shared: in the first \num{5000} conversational requests of
the traces used here, \num{4080885} prompt-block instances deduplicate to
\num{2695220} distinct blocks, a $1.51\times$ factor.

\subsection{H3: HBM plus high-bandwidth flash}
H3~\cite{h3} assumes a B200-class GPU with \SI{192}{\giga\byte} of HBM3e across
eight cubes at \SI{8}{\tera\byte\per\second}, and adds eight HBF stacks of
\SI{384}{\giga\byte} each---\SI{3}{\tera\byte} at comparable aggregate
bandwidth. Two structural points are often modelled incorrectly and matter here:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{No HBM is surrendered.} The HBM cubes remain on the GPU
        shoreline; HBF is \emph{daisy-chained} behind them, with an address
        decoder and router in the HBM base die splitting traffic across one
        unified address space. There is no per-stack bandwidth derate. A
        side-by-side layout that trades HBM capacity for flash models a
        different machine.
  \item \textbf{Latency is hidden, not low.} A \SI{40}{\mega\byte} SRAM latency
        hiding buffer occupying \SI{8.06}{\milli\meter\squared} of a
        \SI{121}{\milli\meter\squared} base die (\SI{6.7}{\percent}) prefetches
        from HBF. The mechanism is \emph{predictability}: layer $N{+}1$'s
        weights are known while layer $N$ computes. It is a read-side buffer; it
        hides read latency, not write latency, and it creates no bandwidth.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Why endurance becomes the problem}
H3 targets read-only data and argues that low write endurance is therefore not a
disadvantage. We target KV blocks that HBM spills on the same hardware. Under
that workload HBF receives a sustained write stream, and TLC NAND at \num{3000}
P/E cycles must absorb it. With ideal wear levelling and a write-amplification
factor of one, each GPU's \SI{3}{\tebi\byte} admits about
$\SI{9.9e15}{\byte}$ of writes over its life. We use this figure only within
ratios and thresholds, never to quote a lifetime in days, because the far tier
has no shared-bandwidth model here.

PCM is a natural staging tier. It is non-volatile and bit-alterable, so it does
not pay NAND's erase-block penalty. Its endurance, however, varies by orders of
magnitude across devices, and no figure is published for the generation whose
density we use (\S\ref{sec:endurance}). We therefore treat PCM endurance as an
open axis.

\subsection{Placement and capacity of the PCM tier}
We place two PCM dies on top of each HBF stack, \emph{within the existing stack
footprint}. PCM does not displace NAND dies laterally and therefore does not
reduce HBF capacity. At \SI{35}{\milli\meter\squared} per die and the
first-generation 3D XPoint density of
\SI{0.62}{\giga\bit\per\milli\meter\squared}~\cite{ehrmann2022pcm}:
\begin{align}
2 \times 35 \times 0.62 &= \SI{43.4}{\giga\bit} \text{ per stack} \nonumber\\
                        &= \SI{5.43}{\gibi\byte} \text{ per stack} \nonumber\\
8 \times \SI{5.43}{\gibi\byte} &= \mathbf{\SI{43.4}{\gibi\byte}} \text{ per GPU.}
\end{align}
The \SI{35}{\milli\meter\squared} die is the area of a \SI{1}{\tebi\bit} TLC
die at \SI{29}{\giga\bit\per\milli\meter\squared}; that density is uncited, so
the die area is an open parameter (\S\ref{sec:limits}). The PCM tier is about
\SI{1.4}{\percent} of the flash capacity it sits on.

\subsection{Data path}
A KV block enters the staging tier \emph{only when HBM evicts it} (spill). A
later request reads a stored block from PCM or from HBF directly into HBM; a
block served from HBF is \emph{not} re-admitted to PCM. When PCM is full, its
least-recently-used victim is either demoted to HBF or dropped, according to
the demotion policy.

\subsection{Write policies and the three configurations}
Two policy knobs define the configurations evaluated throughout
(Table~\ref{tab:configs}).

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Admission write policy.} Under \emph{write-through}, every
        admission to the staging tier also writes the block to flash at once;
        the demotion that follows is a second, byte-identical write. Under
        \emph{write-back}, admission writes only the staging tier, and a block
        reaches flash only if it is demoted.
  \item \textbf{Demotion policy.} Under \emph{always}, every staging-tier
        eviction demotes its victim. Under \emph{if\_read}, a victim is demoted
        only if PCM actually served it at least once while resident; otherwise
        it is dropped.
\end{itemize}

Configuration \cfgb\ is \emph{not} an ``HBF-only'' system. It is a write-through

\cfgb's store-served tokens. Holding the buffer size fixed makes every
comparison a policy comparison on the same hardware.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Simulator}
We extend TokenSim~\cite{tokensim}, a discrete-event simulator for LLM inference
that models request arrival, prefill/decode scheduling, paged KV management and
distributed execution. Its Mooncake store~\cite{mooncake} has a memory tier
(our staging tier) and an offload tier (HBF), each with LRU eviction and a
per-operation latency $t = t_\text{fixed} + \text{bytes}/\text{BW}$; bandwidth
is not shared between operations. Write volume is counted exactly, in blocks
and bytes, per tier. Every behaviour we add sits behind a configuration flag
whose default is the stock behaviour, and with all flags at their defaults the
output is byte-identical to the unmodified simulator.

\textbf{Timing.} Flash reads use \SI{0.1}{\micro\second} at
\SI{8}{\tera\byte\per\second}: H3's latency-hiding buffer folded in as a
\emph{perfect-prefetch upper bound}, which flatters the baseline. Flash writes
use \SI{200}{\micro\second} and \SI{3.0}{\gibi\byte\per\second}, TokenSim's
stock SSD default, which is \emph{unsourced}. PCM timing
(\num{0.1}/\SI{1.0}{\micro\second} read/write,
\num{100}/\SI{20}{\gibi\byte\per\second}) is \emph{assumed}. Our results are
byte counts; timing only shapes the schedule. Removing all store time, or
scaling PCM write timing by $0.25\times$ and $4\times$, moved the baseline's
flash stream and every PCM stream by at most \SI{0.54}{\percent}. That test ran
under the earlier copy-at-admission model and has not been repeated under the
configuration used here.

\subsection{Platform and capacity bookkeeping}
The cluster is eight B200-class GPUs running LLaMA2-70B-GQA
($N_\text{head}=64$, 8 KV heads, $N_\text{layer}=80$, $d_\text{model}=8192$) at
tensor-parallel degree 2 and data-parallel degree 4. Tensor parallelism splits
the KV heads, giving 4 heads per shard and
$4 \times 128 \times 2 \times \SI{2}{\byte} \times 80 = \SI{163840}{\byte}$
per token per shard; a 16-token block is \SI{2621440}{\byte}. KV precision is
FP16 and hardcoded in the harness. FP8 would halve every byte count, so FP16
understates the benefit.

Only the tensor-rank-0 worker of each data-parallel group is scheduled; the
rank-1 shard's stream is an exact mirror and is not simulated. Four GPUs
therefore write to the store, every byte figure is \emph{per tensor-parallel
shard}, and batch is quoted per active scheduler. Ratios and reductions are
unaffected. Pools are sized for the four writing GPUs and split into private
per-GPU partitions, because PCM is in-package on each GPU's own HBF stacks:
\begin{align}
\text{PCM} &= 4 \times \num{17776}~\text{blk} = 4 \times \SI{43.4}{\gibi\byte} \nonumber\\
\text{HBF} &= 4 \times \num{1258291}~\text{blk} = 4 \times \SI{3}{\tebi\byte} \nonumber\\
\text{HBM} &= \num{78643}~\text{blk} = \SI{192}{\gibi\byte} \text{ per GPU.} \nonumber
\end{align}
The per-GPU split is enforced in the store and recorded in every result. Because
TokenSim subtracts model weights from HBM while H3 keeps them in HBF, the HBM
capacity is pre-compensated by the weight size so that exactly
\SI{192}{\gibi\byte} remains for KV.

\subsection{Store model}\label{sec:storemodel}
The store implements the data path of \S\ref{sec:arch} with four settings:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{spill-only entry}: a block is written to the store only when
        HBM evicts it;
  \item \textbf{lookup after the HBM hit}: a new request asks the store only
        for blocks beyond those already in HBM, as in vLLM's KV-connector
        interface;
  \item \textbf{read credit on serve}: \emph{if\_read} credits a block only
        when PCM actually serves it;
  \item \textbf{load the missing span}: only blocks not hit in HBM are loaded.
\end{itemize}
Capacity and partitioning were chosen on hardware grounds; these four were
chosen on modelling grounds. This is the only combination we found in which
the store behaves as a hierarchy and PCM serves real reads. Each alternative
fails measurably:
\begin{itemize}
  \item copying every prompt block into the store at admission (the stock
        behaviour) makes HBM and the store parallel copies, and PCM never served
        a read in 332 such runs;
  \item spill-only entry with a lookup that starts at block~0 serves 0 and 769
        of \num{5000} lookups, because the prompt head stays in HBM and the walk
        stops at the first absent key;
  \item crediting every block a lookup \emph{walks} opens the \emph{if\_read}
        gate with no PCM read at all (\S\ref{sec:corrections}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Workload and replicates}
We replay the full Mooncake FAST'25 conversational (\num{12031} requests) and
tool/agent (\num{23608} requests) traces~\cite{mooncake} at
30~requests/s, with at most 48 concurrent requests per
scheduler. These traces are a \textbf{stand-in}: the target workload is
cache-augmented generation, whose corpus size, sharing factor and transient
volume are not yet fixed. The store holds prompt blocks only; generated tokens
are \SI{2.58}{\percent} and \SI{1.94}{\percent} of all tokens. The traces hash
prompts in 512-token chunks against the simulator's 16-token blocks, so a
continuation cannot reuse its predecessor's partial tail and reported hit rates
are floors.

\cfgb\ and \cfgc\ are single runs; their flash streams move by at most
\SI{0.5}{\percent} under perturbation. \cfgd\ is reported as a range over
$N=4$ runs per trace: the unjittered replay plus three seeds of
\emph{burst-level} arrival jitter, which shifts each arrival burst by up to
$0.05\times$ the minimum inter-burst gap (\SI{16.989}{\milli\second} and
\SI{33.338}{\milli\second}) and rejects any run whose arrival order changes. The
simulator's random seed has no effect on these traces, so it cannot produce
replicates.

\subsection{Metrics and verification}
The primary metric is flash write bytes per request (B/req), per
tensor-parallel shard. All results are work-normalized; we quote no latency,
throughput or lifetime in days (\S\ref{sec:notclaimed}). Alongside it we report:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the \emph{residue-adjusted} reduction, which charges keyed blocks still
        in HBM at the end of the run (never spilled) as if eventually demoted;
  \item prefix-cache hit rate, and a \emph{no-store control} (the same run with
        the store disabled), from which the share of the store's reuse benefit
        each configuration keeps is
        $(h - h_\text{none}) / (h_{\cfgb} - h_\text{none})$;
  \item the share of store-served tokens served from PCM;
  \item every PCM write tagged by source: first-time, re-admission after an
        \emph{if\_read} drop, or re-admission after an HBF eviction.
\end{itemize}
Every campaign is gated on reproducing a previously recorded table to the last
digit. Instrumentation counters are verified to change no other output. In
every \cfgb/\cfgc\ run the write identities
$\text{HBF}_{\cfgb} = \text{admissions} + \text{demotions}$ and
$\text{HBF}_{\cfgc} = \text{demotions}$ hold exactly. Expected outcomes are
written down before the runs, and a failed expectation is reported as failed.

%==============================================================================
\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}
%==============================================================================
Every result is from the full traces in the configuration of
\S\ref{sec:storemodel}, quoted as \emph{conversational\,/\,tool-agent}. The
claim has three parts, which we keep separate on purpose:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item removing write-through halves flash writes: \textbf{measured}, but an
        \textbf{identity} that characterises the baseline (\S\ref{sec:identity});
  \item the staging tier should be non-volatile and in-package:
        \textbf{argued, not measured} (\S\ref{sec:argued});
  \item read-gated demotion: \textbf{measured and non-trivial}, the
        contribution (\S\ref{sec:headline}), with its PCM endurance cost priced
        as a threshold (\S\ref{sec:endurance}).
\end{enumerate}
A same-capacity volatile buffer would write identical flash bytes. The case for
PCM rests on durability and in-package placement, not on write volume.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{The baseline's structural write cost (an identity)}\label{sec:identity}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Write-through costs exactly one extra flash write per admitted block. In every
\cfgb\ and \cfgc\ run,
\begin{equation}
\text{HBF}_{\cfgb} = \text{admissions} + \text{demotions},\qquad
\text{HBF}_{\cfgc} = \text{demotions},
\end{equation}
exactly, and demotions differ from admissions only by the blocks still resident
at the end. Removing the write-through copy therefore halves flash writes
whatever the medium: in the simulator, the staging tier's medium changes timing
only. We present this as quantifying a structural cost of the baseline, not as
a finding about PCM.

Table~\ref{tab:identity} gives the numbers. The halving does not depend on
flash capacity. We fixed the rule in advance: if \cfgb$\to$\cfgc\ under
unlimited HBF stays within five points of the finite-HBF value, the halving is
not driven by HBF capacity. It moves by at most \num{0.01}~points. At the design
capacity, \cfgb\ and \cfgc\ evict 1.5--2.0~M blocks from HBF over the full
traces, but those blocks are cold. Removing the evictions changes flash bytes by
at most \SI{0.09}{\percent} and hit rate by at most \SI{+0.11}{\percent}.
Write-back costs no reuse: \cfgc\ keeps about \SI{100}{\percent} of the
store's reuse benefit.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Why the staging tier should be non-volatile and in-package (argued,
not measured)}\label{sec:argued}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\textbf{The simulator does not support this subsection.} It rests on
write-back semantics and on placement, not on byte counts.

\emph{Durability is the primary argument.} Under write-back, an acknowledged
block lives only in the staging tier until it is demoted. A volatile buffer in
that role would lose acknowledged data on power failure; a non-volatile one
would not. This needs no density figure, and the simulator cannot measure it.
The argument is weaker for a cache than for primary storage: KV blocks can be
recomputed, so their loss costs recomputation time and energy, not correctness.

\emph{Placement is supporting evidence only.} At the sourced first-generation
3D XPoint density, \SI{43.4}{\gibi\byte} per GPU fits on top of the existing
HBF stacks, inside their footprint, and takes no NAND capacity
(\S\ref{sec:arch}). We make no density comparison with DRAM.

\textbf{Writes.} \cfgd\ writes 0.97--1.10\,\% (conversational) and
1.28--1.34\,\% (tool/agent) of \cfgc's flash bytes, dropping
\SIrange{98.7}{99.0}{\percent} of what write-back alone still writes. Against
\cfgb\ the worst-case reduction over the four runs is \SI{99.45}{\percent} and
\SI{99.33}{\percent}.

\textbf{Reuse.} \cfgd\ keeps 53.0--53.7\,\% and 87.8--88.0\,\% of \cfgb's
reuse-hit blocks. That figure must be read against the no-store control:
\emph{with no store at all}, the system keeps \SI{48.3}{\percent} and
\SI{85.8}{\percent}, because most reuse is served from HBM. \cfgd\ therefore
keeps only 9.2--10.5\,\% and 13.9--15.1\,\% of the reuse the store adds; its hit
rate is 9.8--11.2\,\% and 2.3--2.5\,\% above no store.

\textbf{PCM does the serving.} PCM serves 71--77\,\% of \cfgd's store-served
tokens, against 6--7\,\% in \cfgb\ and \cfgc. Every \cfgd\ demotion had a real
PCM-served read (eight runs, difference zero).

\textbf{Where \cfgd's PCM writes come from.} Tagging every PCM write gives
89.5--89.7\,\% and 90.2--90.3\,\% first-time admissions and 10.3--10.5\,\% and
9.7--9.8\,\% re-admissions of blocks that \emph{if\_read} had dropped, with
none after an HBF eviction. Those re-admissions account for 99.6--99.9\,\%
(conversational) and 101.2--101.5\,\% (tool/agent) of the block difference
between \cfgd's and \cfgc's PCM writes. \cfgd\ writes 11.5--11.7\,\% and
10.6--10.7\,\% more to PCM than \cfgc, and that extra is the cost of re-taking
dropped blocks.

\textbf{Reading.} \cfgd\ is the aggressive end of a write/reuse trade-off. It
removes about \SI{99}{\percent} of the flash write stream, keeps a tenth to a
seventh of what the store adds over no store, and carries the reuse it keeps in
PCM. It is not a free improvement.

\subsection{Pricing PCM wear: break-even endurance}\label{sec:endurance}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\cfgd\ moves writes from flash to PCM, so its value depends on PCM endurance.
No endurance figure exists for the PCM generation whose density we use
(Table~\ref{tab:literature}), so we treat endurance as an axis, not a constant.
A tier wears out after
$\text{capacity} \times \text{endurance} / \text{B/req}$ requests, and the
system at its first tier. Per writing GPU, HBF is
$\num{1258291} \times \SI{2621440}{\byte}$ at \num{3000} P/E and PCM is
$\num{17776} \times \SI{2621440}{\byte}$. Both capacities must be on the same
basis; mixing a pooled HBF capacity with a per-GPU PCM capacity is a $4\times$
error. The \cfgb\ buffer is DRAM and never wears out.

Figure~\ref{fig:sweep} and Table~\ref{tab:thresholds} give the result. Two
thresholds mean different things.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Net-positive.} Above about $2.4\times10^{5}$ cycles, \cfgd\
        outlasts \cfgc. Below it, \cfgd's extra PCM writes wear PCM out first;
        at $10^{5}$ cycles \cfgd\ lasts 0.89--0.90$\times$ as long as \cfgc.
  \item \textbf{Break-even.} The break-even endurance
        $E_\text{PCM} = \tfrac{\text{HBF cap}\times3000}{\text{HBF B/req}}
        \cdot\tfrac{\text{PCM B/req}}{\text{PCM cap}}$ is where PCM and HBF
        exhaust their budgets together. Above it, more PCM endurance buys
        nothing, and \cfgd's advantage plateaus at the flash write ratio,
        $90.8$--$103.3\times$ and $74.7$--$78.3\times$ over \cfgc.
\end{itemize}
Below its own break-even of about $2.1\times10^{5}$, \cfgc\ is PCM-limited too,
and lasts \emph{less} than \cfgb.

\textbf{Assumptions.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Perfect wear levelling in both tiers.
  \item Flash write amplification of one.
  \item Every write is a full-block write at both tiers.
  \item Each writing GPU's PCM-to-HBF write ratio equals the pooled ratio.
\end{enumerate}
The first three are \emph{optimistic}; relaxing any of them raises \cfgd's
threshold. The fourth is \emph{untested}, because per-GPU write counts are not
recorded. Published chip-level PCM endurance already absorbs some cell
variability, so a comparison must not add a further levelling penalty on top of
it; that would double-count. The end-of-run residue (3.7--4.0\,\% of would-be
admissions) leaves every threshold within \SI{0.04}{\percent} if those blocks
were demoted at each configuration's own rate, and raises them by at most
\SI{4.1}{\percent} if they were never demoted. HBF also holds the model
weights; without static wear levelling its budget would be about
\SI{2}{\percent} smaller.

\textbf{Against published endurance.} Table~\ref{tab:literature} lists what is
published. Endurance spreads widely across PCM devices because two failure
modes, stuck-SET from elemental segregation and stuck-RESET from void
formation, are both driven by atomic migration~\cite{kim2019mrs}; see
also~\cite{wong2010pcm,burr2010pcm}. Mushroom-type cells
(\numrange{1e6}{1e9}) sit above \cfgd's net-positive threshold and overlap its
break-even. The confined-cell record sits above both.

Our density and endurance figures come from \emph{different generations}. The
\SI{0.62}{\giga\bit\per\milli\meter\squared} density is first-generation 3D
XPoint, and no write endurance has been published for those products: one
evaluation on the real hardware reports that its authors ``could not find any
actual endurance data''~\cite{goetze2020pmem}. This mismatch is why we report
thresholds and leave the comparison open.

\subsection{Variants and the earlier window}\label{sec:variants}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Until the full traces became the headline, results used the first
\num{5000} requests. There the end-of-run residue was \SIrange{8.2}{8.4}{\percent}
and \SIrange{16.7}{17.0}{\percent} of would-be admissions; on the full traces
it falls to \SIrange{3.7}{4.0}{\percent}. Compared by metric, the
\num{5000}-request window agrees:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \cfgb$\to$\cfgc\ is \SI{50.53}{\percent}\,/\,\SI{51.31}{\percent} raw
        (\SI{48.38}{\percent}\,/\,\SI{46.49}{\percent} residue-adjusted);
  \item \cfgb$\to$\cfgd\ is at least \SI{99.36}{\percent}\,/\,\SI{99.12}{\percent}
        ($N=16$);
  \item \cfgd's hit rate is 37.5--39.1\,\% and 6.8--7.7\,\% below \cfgb.
\end{itemize}

Results measured under other store models are labelled as such:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Copy every prompt block at admission} (the stock entry):
        \cfgb$\to$\cfgc\ is \SI{50.81}{\percent}\,/\,\SI{50.99}{\percent}.
        Under this model \cfgd\ is equivalent to no store: with honest
        bookkeeping it writes nothing to flash and serves nothing.
  \item \textbf{Concurrency} (copy-at-admission only): \cfgb\ and \cfgc\ flash
        bytes per request moved by \SI{0.50}{\percent}\,/\,\SI{1.10}{\percent}
        over a $12\times$ batch range. A pre-registered falsification test
        confirmed that HBM eviction did not drive flash writes in that model
        (\SI{+0.00}{\percent}\,/\,\SI{-0.07}{\percent}). Neither result is
        established for the spill-only configuration, where HBM eviction is the
        store's only writer.
  \item \textbf{PCM capacity} (copy-at-admission, shared pool): prefix hit rate
        was flat over a $150\times$ capacity sweep. At twice the design
        capacity, roughly second-generation XPoint density,
        \cfgb$\to$\cfgc\ was \SI{51.10}{\percent}\,/\,\SI{52.18}{\percent}.
  \item \textbf{Shared versus private pools} (copy-at-admission, design
        capacity): \cfgb\ and \cfgc\ flash bytes move by at most
        \SI{0.22}{\percent}. The \cfgd\ difference measured then is not quoted,
        because it was produced by the lookup-bookkeeping artefact
        (\S\ref{sec:corrections}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Corrections, Limitations, and What Is Not Claimed}\label{sec:limits}
%==============================================================================
We state these in full because they bound every number above.

\subsection{Corrections to earlier results}\label{sec:corrections}
Each of the following was reported in an earlier version of this work and is
corrected here.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The earlier headline was a saturated operating point.} It came
        from a concurrency cap of 256, where batch differed between
        configurations by up to \SI{7.7}{\percent} (e.g.\ \cfgb$\to$\cfgc\
        \SI{51.2}{\percent}\,/\,\SI{52.6}{\percent}). It was replaced by a
        batch-matched cap of 48, and later by the full-trace results above.
  \item \textbf{The earlier draft's headline did not match its own table.} Its
        prose quoted $90.3\times$ and $86.4\times$ lifetime improvements from a
        batch-unmatched phase, while its table implied $79.8\times$ and
        $58.1\times$. Lifetime ratios are now quoted only as ranges, paired with
        bytes per request.
  \item \textbf{A lookup bookkeeping bug produced the earlier read-gated
        result.} \emph{if\_read} credited every block a store lookup
        \emph{walked}, including blocks then served from HBM. Under
        copy-at-admission, PCM never served a read in 332 runs, yet \cfgd\
        demoted thousands of blocks. With credit given only on an actual PCM
        serve, \cfgd\ under copy-at-admission writes nothing to flash and serves
        nothing: it is equivalent to no store. Its earlier
        \SIrange{99.6}{99.8}{\percent} reductions are retracted. So are the
        ``one PCM read passes the gate'' mechanism and every analysis of
        \cfgd's flash-write variability built on it (timing arms, replicates,
        concurrency sweep, pooled bound); those describe the artefact, not the
        design. The configuration of \S\ref{sec:storemodel} was adopted in
        response.
  \item \textbf{Only four of eight GPUs write to the store.} The simulator
        schedules only the tensor-rank-0 worker of each data-parallel group,
        but earlier work assumed all eight wrote. As a result, the earlier
        \num{142213}-block PCM pool was \SI{86.8}{\gibi\byte} per writing GPU,
        twice the design; batch figures were half their true value; and
        requests-to-wear-out against \SI{24}{\tebi\byte} of flash were twice
        too high, because only \SI{12}{\tebi\byte} receives the counted bytes.
  \item \textbf{A single shared LRU served all GPUs.} One pool and one LRU
        meant a block admitted by one GPU could evict another GPU's block;
        \SIrange{73.6}{75.9}{\percent} of evictions were cross-GPU. In-package
        PCM is private to its GPU, so pools are now partitioned per GPU.
  \item \textbf{The simulator's random seed does not create replicates.} Runs
        with seeds 0, 1 and 2 are byte-identical on these traces. An earlier
        jitter scheme assumed millisecond timestamps, but the traces have
        whole-second timestamps, so that jitter only reordered requests within
        a burst. It was replaced by burst-level jitter that preserves order.
  \item \textbf{Earlier sizing and topology errors.} A PCM tier once configured
        at about \SI{4}{\gibi\byte}, a $10\times$ error, makes every
        \emph{if\_read} result under it void rather than negative. Hardware
        models that gave up HBM capacity for flash, with an invented bandwidth
        derate, modelled the wrong topology and are superseded.
  \item \textbf{Smaller corrections.}
        \begin{itemize}
          \item ``HBF never evicts'' holds only for the first \num{5000}
                requests.
          \item A claim that a continuation's previous output is a minority of
                what it adds was wrong for the conversational trace, where it
                is \SI{67.3}{\percent}.
          \item A partial-tail sharing option intended to lift the hit-rate
                floor lowered the hit rate instead.
          \item An earlier PCM endurance figure, and the statement that PCM
                endurance is ``orders of magnitude higher'' than NAND's, were
                unsourced. Both are withdrawn.
        \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}
\textbf{The store holds prompt blocks only.} Generated KV has no key in the
simulator and never enters the store. The defensible claim is therefore about
\emph{prefix-cache offload} writes. Generated tokens are \SI{2.58}{\percent}
and \SI{1.94}{\percent} of all tokens on these prompt-heavy traces; a workload
with long generations would change that.

\textbf{The workload is a stand-in} for cache-augmented generation, whose
parameters are open.

\textbf{FP16 is hardcoded.} FP8 would halve every byte count and double every
tier's capacity in blocks, so FP16 understates the benefit.

\textbf{Flash reads are modelled generously.} The perfect-prefetch read
parameters rest on H3's argument for predictable, read-only accesses, not for
demand-driven cache hits. They flatter the baseline.

\textbf{Reported hit rates are floors.} The traces' 512-token hashing prevents
a continuation from reusing its predecessor's partial tail. Comparisons between
configurations see the same trace and are unaffected.

\textbf{Flash write amplification is taken as one.} A NAND-level simulation is
outside the scope of this work. Write amplification above one raises \cfgd's
endurance thresholds.

\textbf{Timing sensitivity was tested only under the earlier model}
(\S\ref{sec:method}), and the per-GPU write-ratio assumption behind the
endurance thresholds is untested (\S\ref{sec:endurance}).

\textbf{End-of-run residue.} Blocks still in HBM when a trace ends were never
spilled. They are \SIrange{3.7}{4.0}{\percent} of would-be admissions on the
full traces. Absolute bytes per request are therefore slightly undercounted in
every configuration; residue-adjusted reductions are reported alongside the raw
ones.

\subsection{What is not claimed}\label{sec:notclaimed}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{No latency or throughput results.} This work tests the
        write-absorption idea on byte counts: how many bytes reach each tier,
        and what reuse survives. The simulator's store timing is a
        per-operation function with assumed or unsourced values, no shared
        bandwidth and no far-tier queueing. That is adequate to schedule the
        replay, but not to support latency or throughput results. The
        configurations are batch-matched at 30~requests/s, and their timing
        differences are not results. Latency and throughput are the next phase,
        on a simulator that models them.
  \item \textbf{No lifetime in days.} Days need a write rate, and a write rate
        needs a shared far-tier bandwidth model, which this work does not have.
        Wear is reported as bytes per request, reductions and endurance
        thresholds. Lifetime ratios are given only as ranges; bytes per request
        is the robust figure.
  \item \textbf{No verdict on PCM wear-out.} The thresholds of
        \S\ref{sec:endurance} are positions against published figures, not a
        claim that PCM will or will not last.
  \item \textbf{No claim that PCM's medium causes the halving} of
        \S\ref{sec:identity}. It is a write-policy identity.
  \item \textbf{No concurrency-invariance claim} for the spill-only
        configuration; it was established only under copy-at-admission.
\end{itemize}

%==============================================================================
\section{Related Work}
%==============================================================================
KV cache management in HBM is well studied. Paged attention~\cite{vllm}
eliminated fragmentation and enabled prefix sharing, and
Mooncake~\cite{mooncake} extended it into a disaggregated store with DRAM and
SSD tiers. We treat that store's offload tier as the object of study rather
than its mechanism.

On the hardware side, H3~\cite{h3} is the direct antecedent and supplies our
topology, capacities and latency-hiding argument. We depart from it in workload
rather than structure: H3 confines flash to read-only data and argues that
endurance is therefore a non-issue, while we keep the structure and test the
write-heavy case it excludes. Flash-level write amplification and garbage
collection would refine our assumption of a write-amplification factor of one;
an SSD-level simulator such as MQSim~\cite{mqsim} addresses them, and that
analysis is outside our scope.

Phase-change memory is surveyed in~\cite{wong2010pcm,burr2010pcm}, and its
cycling endurance, with the failure mechanisms behind its wide spread,
in~\cite{kim2019mrs}. What is new here is the role, a thin non-volatile staging
tier protecting a NAND tier inside a GPU package, and a measured selection
policy whose PCM cost we price against that spread rather than against a
single assumed figure.

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%==============================================================================
A \SI{43.4}{\gibi\byte}-per-GPU PCM staging tier, stacked within the existing
HBF footprint, changes the flash write stream in two ways that must be kept
apart. Removing the baseline's write-through copy halves flash writes, but that
is an identity of write policy: a same-capacity volatile buffer would write the
same bytes. The case for a non-volatile tier rests on durability, which we
argue but cannot measure. Read-gated demotion is the measured contribution. It
removes about \SI{99}{\percent} of the remaining flash writes, and pays for it
with most of the store's reuse benefit, against a no-store control. Its value
depends on PCM endurance: it outlasts write-back alone above about
$2.4\times10^{5}$ cycles, and gains nothing further above
\numrange{1.8e7}{2.5e7}. Published PCM devices span $10^{6}$ to $10^{9}$ cycles
for common cells, with a $2\times10^{12}$ record, but none of those figures is
for the generation whose density we use.

The open items are named rather than papered over:
\begin{itemize}
  \item generated KV does not yet enter the store;
  \item latency and throughput need a simulator that models them;
  \item lifetimes in days need a shared far-tier bandwidth model;
  \item PCM endurance needs a figure for the generation actually used.
\end{itemize}
